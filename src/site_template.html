Confused on Purpose · phase 1 results

Can an AI play a student who gets maths wrong in one specific way?

We put real maths questions to nine language models times, mostly asking them to answer as a student who holds one particular misconception. Two stories came out of it: one about building better tests, and one about how these models handle "pretend you don't know".

1 The problem

Every wrong option on a good test question is a trap with a purpose

Take 1/2 + 1/3. A student who adds the tops and adds the bottoms writes 2/5. Put 2/5 among the options, and the student who picks it has just told you exactly what they misunderstand.

That's what a well-built wrong option (a distractor) does: it turns a wrong answer into a diagnosis. But building these traps is slow, expert work, and checking them is slower. A distractor nobody falls for is dead weight. A question where confused students still find the right answer can't tell understanding apart from luck. Today the only way to find out is to put the question in front of thousands of real students and wait for the data.

2 The idea

Rehearse the question with simulated students first

Tell a language model "You are a student who believes [some misconception]", show it the question, and see which option it picks. Do that for many misconceptions and you get a preview of how a classroom would answer, before a single real student sees the question.

That only works if a model can genuinely hold a misconception on command: make that specific mistake, rather than quietly answering correctly or picking any old wrong answer. This first phase tests exactly that. If it holds up, the second phase is an audit tool built on top of it (see where this goes next).

3 How we tested it

Real questions, expert-labelled mistakes, three ways of asking

We used the Eedi dataset from Kaggle's Mining Misconceptions in Mathematics competition: real maths questions where education experts labelled each wrong option with the misconception that produces it. After setting aside questions that need a picture, that leaves question + wrong option + misconception pairs from questions, covering different misconceptions.

For every pair we asked each model the same question three ways:

Matched persona
"You are a student who believes adds the tops and the bottoms…"

Success is picking the option that misconception leads to. We call that a hit.

Control persona
"You are a student who believes the angles in a triangle add up to 360°…"

Same question, unrelated belief. This tells us how tempting the option is anyway.

No persona
"Answer the question."

Can the model solve it at all? This is the knowledge it has to set aside.

Each persona was asked with two wordings: student ("You are a student who believes…") and teacher ("A student believes…; predict their answer"). The options were reshuffled for every call, with the target spread evenly over A–D. Every model answered with a single letter. Every call was cached and every random choice seeded, so each number here can be reproduced exactly.

6 Where this goes next

From "can it?" to a tool that checks questions before students see them

Phase 1 says a well-chosen model can hold a misconception reliably enough to be useful. Phase 2 turns that into an audit tool for any multiple-choice maths question:

Pick the panel

Find the misconceptions most relevant to the question, from Eedi's catalogue of .

Run the panel

Each persona answers using the setup that won phase 1: a thinking model with the student wording.

Write the report card

Dead distractor: no persona picks it. Leaky key: confused personas still find the right answer. Gap: a common misconception with no option to catch it, plus a suggested replacement option.

Prove it works

On 100 held-out questions, swap a real distractor for a junk one and check the tool flags it.

Who would use it

Assessment publishers QA-ing a whole item bank overnight instead of waiting for field-test data. Question writers getting distractors aimed at specific misconceptions as they draft. Learning platforms turning each wrong answer into "this student probably believes X" and serving the right follow-up.

What we'd still need

A check against real student answer rates: the models reproduce the expert-labelled mistake, but we don't yet know whether they're as tempted as real children are. Also a licensing answer: Eedi's data is non-commercial, so a product would need permission or its own question bank.

1 The question

Can a model un-know something on request?

Ask a model that's good at maths to answer as a student who believes 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5. It knows the answer is 5/6. Can it set that aside and be wrong in exactly the way it was told, or does what it knows keep leaking through?

Whenever a "confused" model misses the intended mistake, the answer lands in one of two places, and they mean very different things:

Success

The intended mistake

It picked exactly the option the misconception leads to.

Failure 1: knowledge leak

The correct answer

It couldn't let go of what it knows and answered correctly anyway.

Failure 2: wrong mistake

A different wrong option

It did let go of the right answer, but couldn't follow the faulty rule to the right wrong answer.

2 How we looked at it

Same experiment, a different lens

The data is the experiment described on the edtech tab: real Eedi maths questions, each wrong option labelled with its misconception, and every model asked with a matching persona, an unrelated (control) persona, and no persona at all. Here we look at where each answer lands, not just whether it hit, and at what happens when the misconception doesn't apply to the question at all.

One comparison matters most: GPT-5.4 with thinking off and on. It's the same model with the same prompt word for word, and only one setting changes: whether it's allowed to reason privately before answering. Its answer is still a single letter, and we never see the reasoning, only how long it was (about 100 tokens on average).

A side note on reading instructions literally

We tried switching on thinking for Claude Sonnet 4.6 too. It did think, but its entire thinking was the answer letter: 7 tokens, every time. Our prompt ends with "Reply with one letter only", and Claude applied that to its private reasoning as well. GPT-5.4 read the same words as a rule about the final answer, and thought for about 100 tokens first. How much a model thinks depends on how it reads the instructions, not just on whether thinking is switched on.

4 What it suggests

Pretending needs room to think, and clear instructions about where to stop pretending

What we can say

  • Models can set what they know aside: with a matching persona, right answers collapse.
  • The leak is a small-model problem. Strong models fail by making the wrong mistake.
  • Private reasoning is the biggest single lever: it moves answers into the intended mistake and out of everywhere else.
  • "Imagine a confused student" can turn into "be confused about everything", especially with the teacher wording.

What we'd test next

  • Visible working: let the persona write out its (wrong) working. Does it compute the right answer first and then steer away?
  • Prompt wording: does dropping "one letter only" let Claude think, and does that close its gap?
  • Real students: are the mistakes models find easy to fake also the ones children make most?
